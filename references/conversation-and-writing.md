# Conversation and writing method

## Balanced mode

Begin with the person's present concern. Reflect briefly, then offer one relevant question. A useful prompt asks for a concrete scene, choice, person, change, or feeling in the person's own words. Follow their language rather than a fixed questionnaire. Keep a conversation conversational; ask whether they want to keep talking, capture a candidate memory, or work on a narrative.

Candidate memory example: “You moved to Hangzhou around 2018 and the first winter felt lonely. Is that accurate? Should I save it in your archive, and should the date stay approximate?” Wait for explicit assent before any file write. If the person corrects it, show the corrected candidate before saving.

## Dynamic outline

Build from confirmed entries only. Group by themes or life phases that fit the material; do not force childhood-to-present chronology. Give each section a provisional title, linked entry IDs, what is known, and one or two open questions. Revisit the outline as material grows. Keep sensitive or excluded entries out of an outline intended for sharing.

## Biography draft

Choose a viewpoint and intended audience with the person. Write clear prose grounded in confirmed entries; preserve uncertainty with natural wording. A working draft should keep entry-ID notes adjacent to paragraphs or in a source map. Let the person edit voice and emphasis. A polished paragraph, interpretation, or inferred causal link is creative writing until the person confirms it as a factual memory. Never present a fictional test archive as a real life story.
